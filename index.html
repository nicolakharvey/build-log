Four ways one person wrote the same line, and the parser that only knew one

I had six years of weekly research documents in an archive and a script that pulled the write-ups out of them. It worked, and it had been wrong for months. Twenty one pages parsed as holding no documents at all while holding plenty, and I only found out because I stopped testing it on a page that already worked and ran it over all six hundred and ninety five.

Four faults, and they are all the same fault. A person writing the same line every week for six years does not write it the same way. Sometimes he numbered them. Sometimes he dropped a word from the heading. Once, for three weeks, he wrote the date the Australian way round in a document that was otherwise American. And the one that had done the most damage: my pattern looked for a literal space in the middle of a phrase, but the text had been reflowed before it reached me, so a line that wrapped arrived with a newline in the middle of that phrase instead. That single space had blanked five straight weeks and I had no idea.

The date one is the one I would have got wrong if I had been in a hurry. The obvious move is to pick an order and apply it. Instead I made the parser read whichever field could not possibly be a month, and for the dates where both could, I checked rather than assumed: every ambiguous date had to fall after the week its page covered and land on the same weekday every time, because these documents run to a fixed weekly deadline. All sixteen resolved the same way. The old code had failed safe rather than filing them wrongly. That is the only reason there was nothing to unpick.

What protected me was one rule. Every change was run over the whole archive and diffed against the previous run before it was kept, and nothing shipped unless zero documents were lost and every altered one had been read by eye. That caught two of my own fixes making things worse. It also caught two mistakes I made while measuring, which are the ones worth passing on, because both invented findings rather than failing.

The first: a table in the source had an unclosed row tag, so splitting on rows swallowed the first entry of every section. My check reported seven records that did not exist. The second: percentages in that table used comma thousands, and my expression read one thousand four hundred and forty nine as four hundred and forty nine. That one manufactured about eleven large discrepancies out of nothing, and I nearly reported all of them.

A parser that throws is a good afternoon. A parser that returns a different answer, confidently, is the one that costs you. Both of mine returned answers. Neither threw.

Across an archive of roughly a thousand documents, coverage went from about 86% to about 95%. Pages parsing to nothing went from twenty one to zero, and every recovered document now attaches to the week it was written in, against one that did not before.

Proof: the before and after diff over all 695 pages, re-run from the committed script.

Limits. The weekday rule resolved every ambiguous date in this archive and is not proof about pages not yet written. The threshold that decides when two entries are one document and not two is calibrated on forty three measurements from a single archive.

The check that catches an incentive pointing the wrong way

I have a rule that the pass and fail conditions for anything I build get written down and locked before a line of code exists, and that whoever builds the thing may fix the work but never touch the checks. I added a rule to it this week, after a build passed thirty one of those checks twice, ran clean, and was still wrong in a way none of them could see.

The game rewards bold play over careful play. That is the whole design. What the checks could not see was that three separate pieces of it were doing the reverse. A bonus multiplier was read after the move resolved, so the bigger the move the smaller its own multiplier. A streak bonus counted pressure but not effort, so the cheapest possible move made under pressure earned what courage was supposed to buy, paying nearly three times as much per move on a third of the work. And the difficulty advanced on a counter that bold play increments more slowly, so the bold player got the easier ride. Every one of those was a defensible line on its own. Together they made timidity the winning strategy, and not one check said a word, because each check asked whether a rule fires and every rule fired perfectly.

The rule I added: an incentive is a relationship between two strategies, so it can only be tested as one. Never gate it with a number, because any number a bold run produces, a timid run can reach by another route. Gate it as a comparison. Simulate the behaviour you want to reward and its exact opposite under identical conditions, and require the one you want to win on the payoff and on surviving. That single comparison found all three at once.

It kept earning its place. When I added a set of levels, the same discipline said fourteen of the twenty four targets were impossible and one of them completed itself if the player did nothing at all. Targets are not set by feel any more. Four different players are simulated on every level, from careful to careless to one who does nothing, and each target has to sit strictly between what the careless one reaches and what a real pair of hands manages. Four goals had to be re-aimed rather than re-numbered, because the simulation showed they were passive: they happened whether or not you played well, and a goal that happens anyway is not a goal.

Two more rules came out of it, both cheap and both now standing. Gate in both directions: play it perfectly and check the state does not run away, then play it not at all and check that it fails, because the second one catches a thing that works and does not matter. And distrust the instrument before you distrust the work, because three separate times the tool told me something the thing itself disagreed with, and reading the thing directly settled it in two minutes each time.

Limits, honestly. Every number here comes from simulated players on my own machine, and nobody has played it on a phone yet. That is the only test that can say whether it is any fun, and I would not claim otherwise until it has happened. The frame timing is a laptop measurement, and a mid range phone is several times slower. It is untested in Firefox.

Proof: muplu.com/pulsemerge. Fifty three checks, run against the live site rather than my copy of it, with the published file compared by fingerprint against the one that passed them.

20 August 2026

The archive that renders perfectly and is empty

A twenty year old website is being retired, so the content had to come off it first. I pulled 1,561 pages, 1,792 images and the stylesheets and fonts, and then spent most of the effort on the part that is easy to skip: proving the copy still works once the original is switched off. Every check you can run casually runs while the old site is still answering. That is the one condition where a hollow copy looks identical to a good one.

Three things would have shipped a broken archive and all three looked fine at the time. The pages lazy load, so the real image sits in a data attribute while the visible one is a blank placeholder, and a saver that reads the obvious attribute stores empty boxes. Every internal link is absolute, about forty a page. Nothing would have clicked through. The styling and fonts live on the domain being switched off.

The one I nearly missed is the one I want to remember. I shorten saved filenames, and the shortening cut the extension off the main stylesheet. The host then served it as a generic download, and because that host sends a header telling browsers to be strict about types, the browser refuses the file outright. All 673 styled pages would have rendered as bare text. The file was present, the link resolved, and every check short of asking the server what type it was returned green.

Two smaller ones worth keeping. Three pairs of addresses differed only by capital letters, and my filesystem treats those as one name, so each was overwriting its twin: six real pages, three of them about to vanish without a message. And I built the address list from two sources rather than one, the site's own index and a public web archive, because each was missing over four hundred pages the other had.

Limits, honestly: the only test that means anything is a count of references still pointing at the old host, and it is zero, but that is a machine reading structure rather than a person reading 1,561 pages. Five pages the site links to were already dead before I arrived and no archive holds them, so they stay as honest broken links rather than something I invented.

Proof: the archive answers with its source treated as gone. Zero images, stylesheets or fonts still pointing at the old host, zero blank placeholders left, zero broken internal links out of 35,218 that resolve inside it, and every referenced file present.

An app that is mostly a beach

My AI influencer's audience got an app today. One minute a day with your dog is the whole ask: you walk, you press a paw into the sand, and something small washes up on a photographed shore, a shell, a feather, sea glass, each one carrying that day's short lesson in reading a dog or settling your own attention. The scene is a real render that follows the actual clock, dawn to stars. Thirty days of curriculum, a shelf that keeps every find, and seven of the finds hold her cloned voice, framed as putting a shell to your ear.

The design rule that shaped everything: no punishment anywhere. A missed day is never mentioned and the count never goes down, because the product is for people worn out by apps that shame them. I took the engagement checklist from my game work and translated it mechanism by mechanism, keeping autonomy and collection, refusing streak-breaking, timers and escalation by name.

It shipped twice. The first version worked and was rejected as flat, the second was rejected as off-brand, and the third is the one that stayed: real photographs, a living scene, the voice pieces. A browser-driven test caught a dead button every static check had passed, and the deploy was proven with a live canary signup, a delivered email, and a clean deletion before anyone was told it existed.

Limits, honestly: the audience is small today, so this is a product bet, not a traction claim; the seven voice pieces went live machine-verified and my own ear came after the ship, not before; and the research finding that no other openly-AI persona has built a ritual app instead of a chatbot of herself is an absence of evidence across targeted searches, not a proven absence.

Proof: the app live on the night beach. Left, the daily choice and the paw press. Right, day one's find and its lesson.

A rule is not a check

I keep this page as a record of what I build, and the instruction to keep it current was written down in three separate places. It was still twelve days behind, and the entry below this one had been finished and left sitting on my machine, unpublished. Nothing had gone wrong with the intention. A rule gets read at the moment it is written and never at the moment it is broken.

The check reads two things that cannot have an opinion, the commit history across every project folder and this page itself, and reports only the gap: which days have work and no entry, and whether an entry is written and not published. It runs at the end of every session and costs about a tenth of a second, because it serves a cached scan and rebuilds it in the background.

It ships with a waiver list. Any day I decide is not worth an entry gets named in a file and silenced for good, since a check that keeps raising things I have already ruled on becomes noise within a week, and a check you have learned to ignore is worse than no check at all.

One limit, and it is printed inside the tool rather than in its documentation: it can only see work that touched a tracked folder, so a render, a video or a one-off page is invisible to it and stays my own judgment.

Proof: this page. The entry below, dated 19 August, is the one it caught written and unpublished, and both went up together.

19 August 2026

Three lip sync engines

My AI influencer is in beta and she needed to speak. I tested three engines against the same still image and the same voice track. The local LTX video rig kept her mouth shut in twelve of fifteen samples. Seedance 2.0, hosted, articulated on every sampled frame and rendered in about two minutes; at 36 credits per 8 seconds of video, short pieces are the affordable shape. LatentSync is built for CUDA, so I ported it to Apple Silicon in six patches. The port ran. That is all it did: 81 minutes for a 7.6 second clip, mouth barely moving.

One caveat, stated because it could change the verdict: the port was fed the clip whose mouth never moved, the hardest job a re-sync model can get. The rematch costs another 81 minutes, so it waits for a reason.

Sampled frames, same image, same audio. Top: LTX, local. Middle: Seedance 2.0, hosted. Bottom: LatentSync, ported to Apple Silicon.
